ICD-10 · Chapter V

Mental & Behavioural Disorders: Organic Conditions

The International Statistical Classification of Diseases (ICD) is a global system listing all diseases and health conditions. Most countries use version 10; some have adopted version 11. This chapter covers organic brain disorders — from dementia to delirium.

Organic Mental Disorders Overview F00–F09

What is Dementia?

Dementia is not a single disease — it is an umbrella term for a progressive decline in cognitive abilities severe enough to interfere with daily life. Think of the brain as a vast library: with dementia, books fall off shelves, labels fade, and eventually the librarian can no longer find anything.

It is not a normal part of ageing. Forgetting where you put your keys is normal; forgetting what keys are used for is not. The most common type is Alzheimer's disease (60–80% of all cases). No cure currently exists, but some medications can slow progression.

Core Signs Across All Types

Memory loss Recent events fade first; distant memories persist longer
Confusion & disorientation Getting lost in familiar places, mixing day and night
Difficulty with daily tasks Cooking, paying bills, getting dressed independently
Personality changes Previously calm people becoming suspicious or aggressive

Alzheimer's Disease F00

Key facts The most common dementia type. Onset is typically insidious — gradual and slow over 2–10+ years — usually appearing between ages 65–70. Family history increases risk.

Symptoms in Detail

Short-term memory loss Forgetting recent information, asking the same question repeatedly
Thinking & planning Difficulty managing budgets, following recipes, abstract reasoning
Language problems Struggling to find words; calling a "watch" a "hand-clock"
Visuospatial difficulties Trouble reading, judging distance, recognising faces
Misplacing things Putting keys in the fridge; may accuse others of stealing
Mood & behaviour Depression, anxiety, suspicion; delusions and hallucinations in late stages

Vascular Dementia F01

Caused by reduced blood flow to the brain, often following one or more strokes. Symptoms closely overlap with Alzheimer's, but the history of onset is tied to vascular events. Formerly called arteriosclerotic dementia.

CodeSubtypeDescription
F01.0Acute onsetDevelops rapidly after a succession of strokes (thrombosis, embolism, haemorrhage)
F01.1Multi-infarctGradual onset after multiple minor ischaemic episodes accumulating infarcts
F01.2SubcorticalDamage in deep white matter; linked to high blood pressure; cortex usually preserved. Severe cases may be called Binswanger's encephalopathy

Dementia in Other Diseases F02

Dementias caused by conditions other than Alzheimer's disease or vascular damage. Can begin at any age, though rarely starts in very old age.

CodeDiseaseKey Features
F02.0 Frontotemporal (Pick's) Usually 50–60 years old. Early gradual personality changes before memory loss; frontal and temporal atrophy; no Alzheimer-type plaques/tangles
F02.1 Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease Rapid subacute progression; caused by a transmissible agent (prion). Death usually within 1–2 years of onset
F02.2 Huntington's disease Autosomal dominant inheritance (50% risk to children). Onset 30s–40s. Early symptoms may be psychiatric (depression, paranoia) before motor signs
F02.3 Parkinson's disease Dementia appearing in advanced Parkinson's; exact relationship to Alzheimer's still unclear; used mainly for research classification
F02.4 HIV-associated dementia Forgetfulness, slow processing, poor concentration. Children develop neurodevelopmental disorder. Progresses rapidly to global dementia in many cases
F02.8 Other specified diseases Dementia as a manifestation of various other cerebral or somatic conditions; underlying condition coded separately

Unspecified Dementia F03

Used when dementia criteria are met but it is not possible to classify the condition into any specific type (F00–F02.9).

Organic Amnesic Syndrome F04

Prominent impairment of recent and remote memory while immediate recall remains intact. New learning is severely reduced, causing anterograde amnesia. Confabulation (unconscious fabrication of memories) may be present. Often linked to lesions in the hippocampus or hypothalamic-diencephalic system. Near-complete recovery is possible if the underlying lesion is treatable.

Delirium F05

Important distinction This category covers delirium not caused by alcohol or psychoactive substances. Drug-induced delirium (e.g. acute confusion from antidepressants in elderly patients) is coded here; the medication should be additionally coded using Chapter XIX of ICD-10.

A transient syndrome of concurrent disturbance of consciousness, attention, perception, thinking, memory, psychomotor behaviour, emotion and the sleep-wake cycle. Most common after age 60. Most cases recover within 4 weeks, but delirium can persist up to 6 months in chronic liver disease, carcinoma, or bacterial endocarditis. A delirious state may be superimposed on — or progress into — dementia.
